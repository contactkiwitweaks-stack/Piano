### 3.1 Damper Pedal Dynamics & Sympathetic Resonance
When the damper pedal (CC64) is depressed, all 88 sets of felt dampers are lifted away from the strings. This produces three fundamental acoustical phenomena:

1. **Sympathetic String Excitation**:
   - Striking a single note (e.g., $\text{G}_2 = 98\text{ Hz}$) does not merely cause string $\text{G}_2$ to vibrate.
   - Every undamped string across the entire harp whose fundamental or partials align with the harmonic series of $\text{G}_2$ begins to vibrate sympathetically:
     - $\text{G}_1$ (Sub-octave fundamental) vibrates at its 2nd partial.
     - $\text{D}_3$ (Twelfth, 3rd partial) vibrates.
     - $\text{G}_3$ (15th, 4th partial) vibrates.
     - $\text{B}_3$ (17th, 5th partial) vibrates.
     - $\text{D}_4$ (19th, 6th partial) vibrates.
   - Result: A single note produces a rich, luminous acoustic halo known as **damper bloom**.
2. **The High-Frequency Cutoff vs. Low-End Saturation**:
   - High treble strings ($\text{C}_6 - \text{C}_8$) have low mass and high tension; their sympathetic vibration is brief ($<1\text{ second}$).
   - Low bass strings ($\text{A}_0 - \text{C}_2$) have enormous mass (copper-wound steel); their sympathetic resonance can sustain for $8\text{ to }15\text{ seconds}$.
   - **Acoustic Rule for Virtuoso Coda**: If the damper pedal is held continuously through fast chromatic passages in the low-to-mid register, the bass strings accumulate energy faster than they can dissipate it. The soundboard enters **overtone saturation**, transforming clear harmony into a catastrophic acoustic roar.

### 3.2 String Mechanics and Inharmonicity in Bass Tenths
A piano string is not an idealized mathematical line; it is an elastic steel wire possessing bending stiffness. This stiffness causes real piano harmonics to be slightly sharp relative to true integer multiples—a phenomenon known as **inharmonicity**:
$$f_n = n f_1 \sqrt{1 + B n^2}$$
where $B$ is the inharmonicity coefficient (highest on short, thick strings).

#### Why Left-Hand Tenths Ring Clearer Than Thirds:
- If a pianist plays a low root and third in the contra-octave (e.g., $\text{G}_1$ and $\text{B}_1$):
  - $\text{G}_1 = 49\text{ Hz}$; partials at $98, 147, 196, 245, 294, 343\text{ Hz}$.
  - $\text{B}_1 = 61.7\text{ Hz}$; partials at $123.4, 185.1, 246.8, 308.5\text{ Hz}$.
  - The high partials of $\text{G}_1$ ($245\text{ Hz}$) and $\text{B}_1$ ($246.8\text{ Hz}$) collide violently, generating rapid, ugly acoustic beats ($\approx 1.8\text{ Hz}$ difference frequency) that sound like muddy, rumbling distortion.
- If the third is transposed up an octave to form an **open tenth** ($\text{G}_1$ and $\text{B}_2$):
  - $\text{B}_2 = 123.5\text{ Hz}$.
  - The fundamental of $\text{B}_2$ lines up cleanly with the 3rd partial of $\text{E}$ or reinforces the clear overtone series, eliminating low-register phase collision.
  - **Acoustic Law**: In High Romantic virtuoso ostinatos, *always* separate bass fundamentals from their chord thirds by at least a tenth ($\geq 16\text{ semitones}$).

### 4.1 Forearm Rotation Mechanics in Tenth Leaps (Pronation & Supination)
The left-hand ostinato in the *Presto con fuoco* requires leaping from a deep sub-bass note ($\text{G}_1$) to a tenth interval in the middle register ($\text{D}_3 + \text{B}\flat_3$) at speeds exceeding 5 leaps per second.
- **The Fatal Flaw of Lateral Stretching**: A pianist who attempts to reach this tenth by stretching the hand sideways while keeping the forearm rigid will develop severe extensor tendon strain within 16 measures, resulting in muscle fatigue, inaccuracy, and catastrophic speed loss.
- **The Zimerman Solution (Forearm Rotation & Parabolic Arc)**:
  1. **Supination (Striking the Bass Root)**: The forearm rotates slightly outward (supination) so that the weight of the arm drops naturally onto the 5th finger (pinky) on the contra-root ($\text{G}_1$).
  2. **The Rebound Arc**: As the pinky strikes, the arm uses the keybed's upward reaction force as a trampoline. The forearm glides in a graceful **parabolic arc** (rising approximately 4 to 6 centimeters above the key surface) traveling upward and to the right.
  3. **Pronation (Striking the Tenth)**: As the hand arrives over $\text{D}_3 + \text{B}\flat_3$, the forearm rotates inward (pronation), allowing the thumb (1) and second finger (2) to drop cleanly onto the chord without finger stretching.
  4. **Cycle Repetition**: The arm immediately recoils in a reverse parabolic arc back to the bass. The physical sensation is not "reaching," but an effortless pendulum swing driven from the elbow and shoulder.

### 4.2 Wrist Shock Absorption and Martellato Chords
Striking double octaves and crashing chords at $fff$ dynamic transfers immense kinetic shock into the human skeleton.
- **The Kinetic Gate**:
  - If the wrist joint is floppy, key strike velocity is lost; the tone sounds hollow and lacks penetrating power.
  - If the wrist joint is frozen rigid, the impact force travels straight into the carpal tunnel and elbow tendons, causing bruising and tendonitis.
- **The High-Performance Shock-Absorbing Wrist**:
  - Zimerman maintains a **dynamic viscoelastic wrist**. At the moment of hammer impact, the hand arch and MCP joints (knuckles) are braced like an architectural arch.
  - The wrist is held firm for the first 10 milliseconds of key strike, transferring the full gravitational weight of the torso into the keybed.
  - Immediately following impact, the wrist flexes slightly downward and upward in a microscopic cushioning wave, absorbing the keybed rebound force while the fingers sustain the notes.

### 4.3 The Physics of the "Drop-Off": Muscular De-Contraction
The most demanding biomechanical feat in Zimerman's 7:40 transition is not playing fast—it is **stopping instantaneously**.
- When playing fortissimo martellato octaves at 7:35–7:39, the antagonist and agonist muscle groups in the pianist’s arms are firing at maximum recruitment.
- To execute the *sotto voce* drop-off at 7:40, the central nervous system must send an immediate **inhibition signal** to the motor cortex:
  - 100% of large-muscle motor units in the upper back, shoulders, and chest must shut off in less than 50 milliseconds.
  - The motor units controlling intrinsic hand muscles (lumbricals and interossei) must immediately activate in rapid, light, micro-twitching firing patterns.
- If a pianist carries even 10% of residual muscular tension across the 7:40 boundary, the *sotto voce* entry will sound clunky, heavy, and stumble rhythmically. The drop-off requires **total physical and neurological stillness** in the intervening silence.
